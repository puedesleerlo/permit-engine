Fact check

Before anything is decided, the system works out what each claim in an application refers to and whether the proof backs it up. It never makes the decision itself.

What it does

An application is a bundle of claims: where the event is, when, how many people will come, what has been arranged. The fact check takes the claims one at a time and does two jobs.

Which park? Which date? Which group?

Words in an application are often vague. “The park” could be one of several. The fact check matches each claim to one exact thing the city knows about: this park, this date, this group. If a word matches nothing, a person decides what it means.

Is there proof?

It then reads the attachments and gives each claim a verdict: supported, unsupported or contradicted. It also says how sure it is, so the rules can tell a strong match from a weak one.

It never decides

The fact check only reports what each claim refers to, whether the proof backs it, and how sure it is. Whether to approve is up to the written rules and, when a case is uncertain, a permit officer.

A worked example: the 5K run

Illustrative. A running charity asks the city events office for a permit: a 5K run in Lincoln Park, 400 runners, two streets closed. The basic checks pass. This is what the fact check finds.

What the three verdicts mean

  • Supported

    The attached proof backs the claim. Four of the five claims here.

  • Unsupported

    Nothing backs the claim. It may still be true, so the city can ask for proof. Here, the police escort.

  • Contradicted

    The proof says something else, such as an insurance letter that names a different park. None here. A contradiction is a red flag: an officer always sees the case.

The fact check stops there. It does not put the case on hold or write to the organizer. The risk tree and the written rules take these verdicts and decide: the case is put on hold, and the city asks once for the police escort letter. If there is no reply, an officer decides. Five parts, one after another

Plain name first, technical name second. Every application goes through all five, in this order.

  1. 01List the claimsClaim graph
    Splits the text into claims, each tied to the proof it needs.
  2. 02Pin down each claimDisambiguation
    Matches each word to one entry in the city’s official list of parks, dates and groups.
  3. 03Weigh the proofEvidence judge
    Supported, unsupported or contradicted, and how sure.
  4. 04Name the kind of caseCase classifier
    Labels the event: type, crowd size, place, length. It does not pick the rules; the rules do that.
  5. 05Hand over the factsAdapter
    Passes the facts to the rules, never a decision.

In technical terms

For technical readers: the record the fact check hands to the rules, how each part is built, and who is responsible for it.

How it is built

The fact check is the system’s world model: the part that works out what is true about the case. It is written in Python. An agentic LLM reads the application, builds the claim graph and judges the evidence. Disambiguation walks a versioned knowledge graph (a SKOS ontology in a triple store) and grades each match: exact, synonym, broader, narrower or none.

The evidence judge is asked three times, and the verdict is weighted by how reliable each type of proof is. The classifier and the adapter are plain code. Every record carries the versions it used.

Who owns it

Owner
The knowledge steward, the person who keeps the city’s facts up to date
Looks after
The city’s terms, how words map to them, and the types of proof
Every change needs
A cited source. It makes a new graph version, is tested on past cases and can be rolled back.
Cannot change
The permit rules or the approval bar. Those belong to the policy owner (the person who signs off on the permit rules).
Asks a person when
A word matches nothing (the steward maps it) or the proof is unclear (a permit officer rules on it)

Why facts are kept apart from decisions

When the two are separate, an auditor or an officer can see exactly where a mistake came from, and fix it in the right place.

Every mistake has an address

When an officer overturns a decision, they first ask whose mistake it was. A misread word or attachment is a fact check mistake. A wrong call on risk or rules is a policy mistake.

Two owners, two kinds of change

Facts are corrected with evidence, by the knowledge steward. Rules change only by authority, by the policy owner. Neither can edit the other’s side.

Any decision can be replayed

Each case records the version of the facts and of the rules it used, and every decision is logged. An auditor can replay any decision later and see what it relied on.

Does keeping them apart make decisions steadier, for example when the same request is written in a pushier style? That is part of what the tests measure. Results pending. How the system is tested.
