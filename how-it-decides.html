How it decides

Every application goes through the same five stages. AI checks the facts and weighs the risk, written rules make the call, and a person decides anything uncertain.

Five stages, from application to decision

Cheap checks run first. AI is used only where reading and judgement are needed, and written rules always make the call.

  1. 01ApplicationThe organizer describes the event and attaches proof.
  2. 02Basic checksNo AI. Fee paid? Park and date allowed? Crowd under 500?
  3. 03Fact checkAI. What does each claim refer to, and does proof back it?
  4. 04Risk assessmentAI and rules. How likely is a problem, and how bad would it be?
  5. 05DecisionRules. Approve, ask for proof, propose a denial, or send to an officer.

Inside the risk step

How the system weighs likelihood and harm, shown for the 5K run.

The risk step works like a formal risk assessment. It asks two things: how likely is a problem, and how bad would it be?

It does this with a decision tree, a short series of yes-or-no questions about the event. AI answers each question from the application and the fact check, and says how sure it is. The tree then leads to a risk level.

The risk level sets the bar. The higher the risk, the stronger the evidence must be. For the 5K run the risk is medium and the police escort is unproven, so the evidence falls short and the city asks for the letter.

In technical terms: the risk tree

TypeSafe Jev answers each question as a typed yes or no with a probability. Each answer is asked three times, to see how stable it is. Judgment questions run as small TypeSafe Jev trees; the fixed checks and the final decision run in Open Policy Agent (Rego). Every check cites the clause it comes from in the city ordinance (the local law that sets permit rules). The case file keeps the versions of the AI, rules and thresholds used, so any decision can be replayed later.

Who does what

Each part has one job. AI gathers and scores the evidence, written rules decide, and people handle what the rules cannot.

We call the fact check the world model (what is true about the case) and the risk tree plus rules the policy model (what to do about it).

AI

Reads, checks and scores. It never decides.

  • Reads the application and its attachments.
  • Works out exactly what each claim refers to: which park, which date, which group.
  • Marks each claim as supported, unsupported or contradicted by the evidence.
  • Answers the risk questions and says how sure it is of each answer.
Built with
An agentic LLM reads applications and checks facts. TypeSafe Jev, an AI tool that answers yes-or-no questions with a confidence score, scores likelihood and impact.

Written rules

Make every automatic decision, the same way every time.

  • Run the basic checks before any AI is used.
  • Hold the permit policy as hard rules, plus simpler checks that the evidence is enough to allow the permit.
  • Approve, ask for proof, or propose a denial for an officer to sign.
  • Send anything uncertain to a person.
Built with
Python, and Open Policy Agent (Rego) for the permit rules.

People

Decide what the rules cannot, and own the rules.

  • At launch, an officer checks every decision. Once a type of decision passes its tests, officers decide uncertain cases, sign every denial and check 1 in 10 automatic approvals.
  • Their rulings tune how cautious the system is.
  • Only the policy owner (the person who signs off on the permit rules) changes the rules. Every change is tested on past cases and can be undone.
  • Every decision is logged, so it can be audited.
Built with
Calibration statistics (isotonic regression or Platt scaling) on officers’ rulings.

The bar for approving on its own

The system approves without a person only when the evidence clears a bar, and the bar rises with the stakes.

What ‘strong enough’ means

Before the system approves anything on its own, it must be sure enough that every requirement is met.

How sure depends on what is at stake. The more harm a wrong approval could do, the more sure it must be. When it is not sure enough, it asks for proof or sends the case to a person.

In technical terms: the approval bar

Each requirement gets a probability that it is met. These scores are calibrated on officers’ past rulings (isotonic regression or Platt scaling), so that a score of 0.9 should mean right about nine times in ten.

Every requirement must hold, so the chance that an approval is right is the product of those probabilities. The risk level sets the threshold it must clear (in the demo, 0.6 for low risk and 0.8 for medium risk). Below it the case is not approved: if proof is missing the city asks for it; otherwise it goes to an officer. High-risk cases always go to an officer.
