Permit Engine

AI decisions you can audit and correct

Governments and companies handle thousands of permit, benefit and loan applications. AI could speed them up, but no one will hand a decision to a system that can't explain itself or be corrected. Permit Engine splits each decision into checkable steps.

  1. Check the factsAI reads the application, pins down what each claim means and checks it against documents and city records.
  2. Assess the riskA yes/no risk flowchart (a decision tree) rates how likely and how harmful problems are, with a confidence score for each answer.
  3. Apply the rulesThe permit policy is written as clear rules that check whether the evidence is enough to approve.
  4. People decide the hard casesUncertain cases and every denial go to an officer. Their rulings tune how cautious it is.

When does an officer see a case? When the system is unsure, when a red flag fires, for every denial, for 1 in 10 automatic decisions, and for every appeal.

What the system learns from. Only rulings where the officer finds the system was wrong, or confirms it was right. Officer mistakes, ordinance changes and one-off cases never teach it.

The headline test. The same request written four ways should get the same decision. If a pushy letter gets a yes that a plain one does not, that counts as an error.

World model: what is the case?

Turns the applicant's words and attachments into facts the policy can use. It never decides what should happen. Anything in it can be corrected with evidence, so a knowledge steward owns it.

Policy model: what do we do about it?

Takes the case facts and decides, citing a clause for every check. Fixed checks run in Rego; checks that need judgment run as Jev trees. Anything in it changes only by authority, so the policy owner owns it.

Decision function

How the case facts become one decision. Eligibility is conjunctive: every applicable condition must hold, so strong proof on one requirement cannot make up for a missing one. The weighted sum lives in the impact score, where trading one factor against another makes sense.

Calibration step

Turning Jev agreement into a probability

  1. Collect. Each spot-checked case gives, for every judgment check, the raw score s and the officer's ruling on that condition.
  2. Fit. Per check type, an isotonic map from s to how often the officer found the condition met. With few rulings, a two-parameter curve (Platt scaling) instead.
  3. Test. On held-out rulings, calibration error must not get worse, and replay shows which past decisions would flip.
  4. Release. The map ships as a versioned item through the shared core, and each case logs the map version it used.

Why spot checks only. Unsure cases and denials are not a random sample of cases, so fitting on them would bias q.

Cold start. With q capped at 0.9, a case with six judged conditions reaches at most p = 0.53. Officers see nearly everything at first, and the system decides alone only as its calibration earns it.

Independence. The product assumes conditions are independent given the evidence. When one document backs two conditions, p comes out too low, which errs toward review.

Which versions apply. Rules and parameters are pinned to the submission date, including for appeals. New proof uses the current knowledge graph and calibration, and every reassessment logs both.

Re-entry. New proof reruns the world model only for the claims it touches, then the whole decision function. After two rounds of asking, the case goes to an officer instead.

Stop switch. Every case in Assessing moves to Officer review, and nothing is issued automatically until it is lifted.

Learning and escalation core

Both models produce the same three kinds of items: escalations, change proposals and lessons. What differs per item is who owns it and how it is checked. So the recommendation is one shared core, with the owner and the validator plugged in per kind of change.

Why shared. One failure often needs a world fix and a policy fix together. A shared core keeps them in one item, one replay and one history.

Why plug-ins. World changes are checked against evidence, policy changes are approved by authority. That difference lives in the kinds table, not in two separate systems.

Reusable. Nothing in the core knows about permits. A new domain brings its own ontology, rules and kinds; the core, log and review screen stay the same.
