Learning and oversight

People stay in charge. Officers decide the cases the system is unsure about, their rulings teach it when to be more careful, and only the policy owner can change a rule.

What reaches an officer, and what they do

At launch, an officer checks every decision. Once a type of decision passes its tests, officers decide uncertain cases, sign every denial and check 1 in 10 automatic approvals. Before the system learns anything, the officer settles whose mistake it was.

Three people, three jobs

Permit officer
Decides unsure cases, signs every denial and spot-checks 1 in 10 automatic approvals (the starting rate; the slowest learning loop can change it).
Policy owner
The only person who can change a rule, an exception, or how far the approval bar may move (the bar for approving on its own, also called its threshold).
Knowledge steward
Corrects terms and facts, with sources. Cannot change the rules or the approval bar.
  1. 01What reaches an officerEvery unsure case, every denial, 1 in 10 automatic approvals and every appeal.
  2. 02How the officer checksReads the original application and attachments, not the system’s summary.
  3. 03Whose mistake?The system’s, the officer’s, a changed ordinance (the local law that sets permit rules), or an odd one-off.
  4. 04If the system was wrongNames the kind of mistake: a misread fact, or a misjudged policy.
  5. 05What happens nextThe ruling goes to the person who owns that part of the system.

Whose mistake was it?

Settled first, so the system only learns from its own errors and its own successes.

The system was wrong
Fix the system. The ruling teaches it.
The officer was wrong
Train the officer. The system learns nothing from it.
The ordinance changed
Update the rules, through the policy owner.
An odd one-off case
Keep it on file. The system does not learn from it.

If the system was wrong, where does it go?

Facts are fixed with evidence. Rules are changed by authority. Any ruling that finds the system right or wrong also adjusts how cautious it is on similar cases.

Spot checks are blind. On the 1 in 10 approvals it checks, the officer decides first and only then sees the system’s answer, so the system cannot nudge the ruling.

Pressure is kept out of the decision. The system scores how pushy a letter sounds (urgency, repetition, “I know the mayor”). Officers never see that score, and it never counts toward a decision.

The load stays human-sized. No more cases reach officers than they can handle. See how the fact check works and how the risk tree and rules decide.

The 5K run at this step (illustrative example)

The charity’s 5K in Lincoln Park is on hold because nothing proves the police escort. The city asks once for the escort letter. If there is no reply, the case goes to an officer, who reads the original application and attachments and decides.

If the officer agrees the escort was never proven, the ruling confirms the system. If the officer finds the system misread an attachment, that becomes a fact-check mistake for the knowledge steward.

Making confidence honest

When the system says it is 90% sure, it should be right 9 times in 10. Officers’ spot-checked rulings are how we check that, and correct it.

Officers’ rulings teach the system two things. How much to trust each AI score is learned only from the random 1 in 10 spot checks. How cautious to be is learned from any ruling that says the system was right or wrong.

For every judgment, such as “is this letter real proof of a police escort?”, the system asks TypeSafe Jev, an AI tool that answers yes-or-no questions with a confidence score. A raw score is not yet a promise. Calibration turns it into one: past scores are compared with what officers actually ruled, and adjusted, so that a score of 90% means officers agreed about 9 times in 10.

Only spot checks tune the confidence scores. Officers check 1 in 10 automatic approvals, picked at random. Unsure cases and denials are left out of this step on purpose: they are not a random sample, so learning from them would skew the scores. Other rulings (unsure cases, denials, appeals) can still move the approval bar and reveal repeated mistakes.

A safe start. Until it has a track record, no single judgment may claim more than 90%. The scores multiply, so an application with six conditions to judge starts at most at about 53%, below the example approval bars (60% for low risk, 80% for medium risk). Even after automatic approvals are switched on, this cap means officers still see nearly every case at first, and the system decides on its own only where its record earns it.

Highest possible starting score, by number of judged conditions: 1 condition 0.90, 2 conditions 0.81, 3 conditions 0.73, 4 conditions 0.66, 5 conditions 0.59, 6 conditions 0.53. Each judged condition is capped at 0.9 at the start, and the scores multiply. Arithmetic from the design, not a test result.

In technical terms: calibration

For each type of check, an isotonic regression maps the raw Jev score s to q, the probability the condition is met, fitted on spot-checked officer rulings. With few rulings, Platt scaling (a two-parameter curve) is used instead. A new map ships only if calibration error on held-out rulings does not get worse, and a replay shows which past decisions it would flip.

Eligibility p is the product of q over the applicable conditions. That assumes the conditions are independent; when one document backs two conditions, p comes out low, which errs toward review. Each case logs the map version it used.

Three ways it learns, at three speeds

Every case, every week, every 50 cases

Each loop learns something different, at its own pace. None of them can rewrite the permit rules on its own.

  • Every case: are we deciding right?

    When an officer overturns a decision, the system raises or lowers its approval bar. It decides on its own less often on cases like that one, and more often where officers keep agreeing with it. The rules stay the same.

  • Every week: are we using the right rules?

    When the same mistake repeats, such as food trucks with no fire permit, it becomes a cautious check that can only ask for proof or send the case to an officer. Only the policy owner can make it a rule.

  • Every 50 cases: are we learning the right way?

    Tunes how the learning runs: how fast the approval bar moves and how many approvals officers spot-check (1 in 10 to start), tested on cases it never saw. It never changes how its own success is measured, or the red flags.

What never teaches it

Officer mistakes, ordinance changes and one-off cases never teach the system. Over time, it decides on its own more often only where officers keep agreeing with it.

What no loop can do. Change a rule without the policy owner, change how its own performance is measured or the red flags the city set, or go live without first being replayed on past cases.

In technical terms: the three loops

The loops are single-loop learning, double-loop learning and learning how to learn. Loop 1 nudges thresholds and impact weights inside bounds the policy owner sets; a larger move needs the owner. Loop 2 turns repeated, officer-confirmed mistakes into lessons that run as cautious checks until the owner promotes them, and flags it if pushy letters start winning more often. Loop 3 tunes the order of work (free checks first, the AI only when needed), the Loop 1 learning rate and the spot-check rate, and tests each change on cases it never saw.

One review process for every change

Every proposed change, to a fact, a rule or the approval bar, goes through the same five steps and the same log. Nothing goes live until it has been replayed on past cases.

  1. 01RouteReads what kind of change it is and assigns an owner and a deadline.
  2. 02ValidateChecks the source and evidence, or replays the change on past cases.
  3. 03ApproveThe owner decides. Nobody can approve their own change.
  4. 04ReleaseA new approved version goes live, with a way to roll it back.
  5. 05MonitorWatches overrides, appeals and outcomes for the part that changed.

What every change carries

Replayed on past cases first: it must fix the mistake without breaking others.

A version number
So any past decision can be replayed exactly.
An owner
The person accountable for the change.
A rollback path
Load the previous version to undo it.
A recheck date
When someone must look at it again.

One log that is never edited

Every item, routing, replay report, approval, release and rollback is written to one append-only event log, with the fact-check and policy versions involved. Entries are added, never changed.

This is what an auditor reads, and what lets anyone rerun a past decision with exactly the versions it used. Owners and auditors share one review screen: what is pending, and over time, errors by kind, overrides and days to fix.

Why one shared review process, not two

The fact check and the risk and rules side send people the same three kinds of work: cases sent up to a person, proposed changes, and lessons (repeated mistakes turned into cautious checks). Only the owner and the check differ.

One failure, one history

A single mistake can need a fact fix and a rule fix together, such as a new term and a new rule. One shared process keeps both in one item, one replay and one history, instead of two queues and two audit trails.

Different checks, one process

Fact changes are checked against evidence. Rule changes are approved by someone with authority. That difference lives in the table of change kinds, not in two separate systems.

Reusable beyond permits

Nothing in the review process knows about permits. A new domain, such as loan exceptions or insurance claims, brings its own terms, rules and kinds of change. The process, the log and the review screen stay the same.
