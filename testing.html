How the data was generated

Every application is synthetic: 400 street-race requests for a fictional city with an invented ordinance, written by a script. No real applicant or event is involved. The same seed gives the same 460 cases, byte for byte, and a hash checks that.

  1. 1

    Draw the facts

    Case card

    From one seed (20261108), a script draws each race’s facts at a set mix: its crowd, its route, its plans. Reality and evidence are drawn separately, so a race can have an escort arranged and no letter on file, or a letter that names another date.

  2. 2

    Set the labels

    Labels

    A hidden truth table sets each tree’s risk level from the real facts, not from the documents. Then 8% of levels move one step, to imitate experts who disagree. Risk is likelihood times severity, summed, and a case is labelled risky at 0.18 or more.

  3. 3

    Write the documents

    Application

    The facts are written out as a form, a cover letter and attachments, using families of written variants. An adequate plan and an inadequate one differ in what they say, and no document holds a label.

  4. 4

    Plant the traps

    Application

    Five patterns are planted to test the reading stages: insurance only asserted, insurance for another venue, a broker’s letter, a police letter for another date, and instructions hidden in an attachment.

  5. 5

    Split, and add tones

    Splits

    Requests are split into 180 train, 120 validation and 100 test, by request, so the tones of one request never cross a split. 20 test requests are rewritten in 3 more tones, making 60 extra cases and 460 in all.

  6. 6

    Check the balance

    Balance report

    A balance report stops with a failure if the mix misses its targets: about 20% labelled risky (20% here), at most about 15% at the severity ceiling, about 50 approvals to expect in the test (59 labelled approve here), and at least 15 training cases on every leaf.

Dataset hash e386801ed659b18e. The right route across all 400 requests: approve 233, denial 100, officer 59, returned 8.

What surrounds the engine during a test

The test bench is a separate system. It writes the cases, holds the answers, and scores the engine. The reading stages and the pipeline never see the case cards or the labels. Only training, threshold choice and scoring read the labels. In this demo, the bench also plays the officer.

Dashed boxes belong to Permit Engine or sit outside both systems, and their full picture is on the Architecture page. Evaluation also asks the LLM and Jev directly, for the two comparison arms.

The truth table reads the real facts, and the trees can only read the documents. That is why a branch like “no proof of an escort” holds a real mix of outcomes, and why its weights have something to learn.

The hidden files: what the reading models and the pipeline never see

How those labels add up

  • Traffic conflict: likely 0.3 × serious 0.4 = 0.12
  • Crowd pressure: possible 0.1 × serious 0.4 = 0.04
  • Medical emergency: possible 0.1 × serious 0.4 = 0.04

The sum is 0.20. A case is labelled risky at 0.18 or more, so this one is. All requirements are met, so its right route is officer.

What the system did with it

Risk 0.210, inside the review band of 0.185 to 0.216. The risk is inside the review band, so an officer decides. The simulated officer ruled “deny”, and the labels say it is risky. Follow it step by step or open it in the replay.

The experiments

What was tried, and what each one changed. A number here is read from that experiment’s saved result when the site is built, and the build stops if the result is gone.

Before building: simulations

These replace the LLM and Jev with noisy answers and make no model calls. Each settled a question before any model was called. One script reruns them all, and checks each still matches its saved result.

  • Can leaf weights learn from labelled cases at all?

    How
    Simulated cases, with noisy answers in place of the models, across training sizes.
    Found
    On 180 training cases, loss fell from 1.240 with the first guess to 0.471 once trained, against 1.053 with shuffled labels. A tree missing a question showed the highest leaf entropy, 0.694 against 0.460 for the next. Adding the question took its loss from 0.799 to 0.576.
    So
    The design went ahead with 180 training cases, and uses leaf entropy to flag a tree that may be missing a question.

  • How many cases do thresholds need?

    How
    The same simulation at different validation sizes, watching the risky-approval target.
    Found
    With 30 validation cases, risky approvals ran over 10% in 33% of seeds. With 120, in 7%.
    So
    The validation split has 120 requests.

  • How should a correction move the weights?

    How
    Backprop and a Bayesian network, compared with settings fixed in advance, in a stable world and after one tree stops being true. An earlier run that tuned settings on post-shift data favoured backprop, and was replaced.
    Found
    After 60 corrections in a stable world, all-tree loss was 0.468 with none, 0.478 with the network and 0.572 with backprop at its larger step. After a shift, backprop repaired the shifted tree further (0.709 against 1.090, from 1.784), while the network had the lower loss over all trees (0.587 against 0.612).
    So
    One Bayesian network. It did less harm when nothing had changed, which is the world this demo plans, and it absorbed a poor namer where backprop got worse than doing nothing.

  • Where should the severity ceiling sit?

    How
    The full decision table simulated at four ceilings, counting the share of cases reaching each row.
    Found
    At a ceiling of 0.85, 42.8% of cases were approved, with 4.9% of those labelled risky. At 0.95, 56.7% and 8.5%.
    So
    A ceiling of 0.85, and a case mix set so about 15% of cases at most reach it.

  • What pass rule can a test this small support?

    How
    Each candidate rule run on 300 simulated tests of the planned size, for a working system, for thresholds that are too loose, and for failed training.
    Found
    The first rule passed a working system only 58% of the time. The chosen rule passes it 83% of the time, loose thresholds 17%, and failed training 2%.
    So
    The rule was changed before the test opened. See the rule.

  • What should a plot of learning start from?

    How
    Reviews simulated from trained trees, and from the first guess.
    Found
    From trained trees, 60 corrections moved loss from 0.468 to 0.478: a flat line. From the first guess, from 1.235 to 0.757.
    So
    The replay starts from the first guess, and its weights have no test result. The sealed test measures trained weights instead.

Before building: probes of the real Jev

Small runs of the real Jev on synthetic cases. Their raw answers are saved, and they are not rerun, because a new call may answer differently.

  • Could Jev point at the wrong tree, in place of an officer?

    How
    Real Jev, on synthetic cases, asked which tree was wrong and what level was right.
    Found
    It flagged a truly wrong tree 4.8% of the time with no shift, and 21.7% after one.
    So
    An officer names the tree and level. Jev does not.

  • Do the trees beat simply asking Jev for each level?

    How
    Real Jev asked directly on synthetic cases, given true facts, beside trained trees given noisy ones.
    Found
    Jev asked directly had loss 1.385 and picked the right level 45.6% of the time. Trained trees had 0.460 and 86.8%. Jev has never seen the author’s truth table, so this says little about which is closer to real risk.
    So
    Jev answers narrow questions. Asking it directly is reported beside the sealed test, and is left out of the pass rule.

On the built system, with real model answers

The real LLM and Jev read every synthetic application once, and their answers are stored. Everything here reads those stored answers.

  • Do the models read the documents correctly?

    How
    All 460 cases compared with the hidden case cards. The 16 that stopped at basic checks were never read, and they count as wrong wherever the card expects a verdict.
    Found
    Verdicts matched the hidden facts on 97.4% to 99.6% of cases, by kind of proof. Jev’s answers fell on the right side of 0.5 on 94.1% to 100%.
    So
    The reading stages were kept, and their stored answers feed everything after them.

  • Which reviews should teach the trees?

    How
    On real stored answers, 100 reviews from a simulated officer at 90% accuracy, read on 90 cases set aside. Mean of 5 officer seeds.
    Found
    Teaching only from disagreements took loss from 0.748 to 0.798, and right level from 70% to 73%. Teaching from every review took them to 0.458 and 86%. A system that learns only from its mistakes gets a skewed view of the cases it sees.
    So
    Every review teaches. This replaced the plan to teach from corrections alone.

  • Does the system notice a question that is missing?

    How
    The hidden truth table raises traffic likelihood at 3 or more major crossings, and the first traffic tree does not ask. The gap was planted, and the fix was written in advance.
    Found
    Traffic likelihood loss on validation fell from 0.808 to 0.479 with the question added.
    So
    The policy owner released it, and the sealed test covers that version. See how a release works.

  • What does Jev add?

    How
    Each Jev question replaced by its mean over validation cases, counting the decisions that change. Near zero would be a valid finding.
    Found
    Decisions that changed: crowd plan adequate, 5 of 115; medical plan adequate, 3 of 115; narrow venue, 0 of 115; traffic plan adequate, 4 of 115.
    So
    Few decisions on these cases hang on any one Jev answer, and one question changes none. Jev stays, and this limit is stated here.

  • Should a case go part way down both branches of a tree?

    How
    Part-way routing compared with one branch only, on validation cases.
    Found
    Loss was 0.464 part way and 0.454 one way only, with the same share approved and the same precision.
    So
    Part-way routing brought no measured gain on these cases. The system still uses it, and this result is reported with the others.

  • Does a pushy cover letter change the outcome?

    How
    20 requests, each rewritten in three more tones, compared with the plain letter and with the plain letter read twice.
    Found
    0 of 60 decisions changed. Evidence readings changed on some requests, out of 20: insistent 6, the mayor letter 2, persuasive 3. A second reading of the same plain letter changed 3.
    So
    See the flips by tone, and four real letters.

  • Do trained trees do better on cases they never saw?

    How
    The sealed test: 100 requests, one look, with a ledger written before their labels are opened.
    Found
    The verdict and every interval are in the results.
    So
    This is the result the rest of the work leads to. It is about the mechanism on these labels, not about real events.

What counts as passing

All three must hold on the sealed test, or the experiment is published as a fail.

  • Lower loss

    Trained trees have lower loss than the first guess and than the shuffled-label control, with each 95% interval on the difference excluding zero.

  • More approvals

    Trained trees approve a larger share of cases automatically than the first guess, with a 95% interval on the difference excluding zero.

  • No clear safety failure

    It fails on safety only if the test clearly shows risky approvals above 5%: the lower end of the 95% Wilson interval on the share labelled risky among approvals is above 5%.

What this rule accepts

In simulation, on the same sizes, this rule passes a working system about five times in six, so a system that works can still fail. It also lets some thresholds that are too loose through. It catches only a clear safety failure, because the test is too small to confirm a 5% target. Those figures are from a simulation with a planted truth, not from this test.

What this test cannot show

About 3 risky approvals in 52 gives a Wilson interval of roughly 2% to 16%, so this test cannot confirm a 5% target. It can only show when risky approvals are clearly above it.

Reading the same letter twice can also change an answer, so flips near the noise floor cannot be told apart from that. With so few requests, even zero flips leaves a wide interval.

What these sizes cannot show

The report states these limits, and so does this page.

  • About 3 risky approvals in 52 gives a Wilson interval of roughly 2% to 16%, so the test cannot confirm a 5% target. It can show only when risky approvals are clearly above it.
  • The labels are the author’s. The trees ask about the same factors as the truth table, so training is close to guaranteed to learn it. Beating the control shows the labels matter. It says nothing about real events.
  • One run is one run. The replay’s curve is read on far fewer cases than the simulations behind this design, so it need not rise at every step.
  • Only labels the author did not write, from real outcomes or an independent expert, could show whether the trees or Jev’s own judgement is closer to real risk. None exist here.
